# Fiction scenes

Scene-level craft for [`writing-draft`](../SKILL.md). The outline said what
each scene must do (goal, conflict, turn, exit state); this file is how the
prose actually does it. The through-line: the reader wants to be _inside_ the
story, behind the eyes, with the clock running.

## Scene vs summary

Summary compresses time and reports: _She worked the till all winter._ Scenes
happen in real time: people want things, get opposed, and change. The rule:
**turns happen in scene** — anything that changes a relationship, a plan, or a
belief gets real-time treatment. Travel, repetition, and aftermath get summary.
A scene card marked `summary` in the outline stays summary; a scene with a turn
stays a scene.

## Scene and sequel rhythm

- **Scene**: goal → conflict → turn (disaster, cost, or reversal). In scene,
  the protagonist pursues the scene goal concretely.
- **Sequel** (short or long): reaction (emotion) → dilemma (weigh the options)
  → decision (new goal). Sequels let a blow land; a story of unbroken scenes
  becomes an event log, a story of only sequels becomes a diary.
- Long reaction beats after small blows; short or absent ones when the plot is
  sprinting. Pacing is the ratio, not the speed.

## Cause and effect inside the scene

**MRU (motivation–reaction unit)** — the atom of in-scene prose: stimulus →
response, in that order. The character feels (body first: the stomach drops),
then thinks, then acts or speaks. Reversing to action-then-reason reads as
robotic; skipping straight to action in a hot moment reads as unfeeling.

- `The rope snapped. She heard it sing before she felt the fall.`
- Keep the order: **stimulus → visceral → thought → action** — and vary it for
  rhythm once the reader has the pattern.

**Therefore / but** holds _inside_ scenes too: each beat of a scene causes or
opposes the next, never merely follows it.

## Narrative distance (the dial)

Closest: unfiltered sense and thought, free indirect style — the character's
idiom inside narration (_Great. Grand. The elevator was broken again._).
Farther: _she wondered whether..._ Furthest: reportage of events. Move the dial
deliberately: closest at cost, wider between scenes. The one distance mistake
that matters — **drift**: mid-scene slides from inside a head to across the
room, usually via filter words and camera-summaries, exactly when the moment is
hottest.

Interiority is where fiction lives: what it's _like_ to be this person now.
Interiority ≠ monologue: a phrase of thought woven into action (_She counted
the bills again. Fourteen. Still fourteen._) beats paragraphs of reported
feeling.

## Dialogue

- **Every line wants something.** People speak to get or keep something; lines
  with no want are small talk, and small talk earns its place only as text
  over subtext (polite words, visible war).
- **Subtext**: on the page, people circle what they can't say. The scene's
  real subject should approach the characters' mouths and swerve — dialogue
  that names the conflict outright is a press release.
- **Tells, not labels**: idiolect (length, register, one recurring tic) before
  adverbs on tags. `said`/`asked` are invisible — that's why they're used.
- **Reactions cost turns**: the line after the important one often should be
  silence — a beat of business — instead of an answer.

## Exposition and grounding

The reader needs to _know_, not be _told_. Moves that keep the story moving
while it grounds:

- **In motion**: establish the world while someone is doing something that
  matters; facts land as consequences, not as a lecture.
- **Point**: put exposition in a character with an angle on it — memory,
  grievance, professional habit — and the facts arrive wearing attitude.
- **Grounding discipline**: no term, place, custom, or rule of the world may be
  _used_ before the prose has established it. Every new established fact goes
  to the bible the same session (`writing-bible`); it is easier to spend canon
  than to re-earn it.
- **The iceberg**: the bible may hold ten times what shows; the prose shows
  the crest and implies the mass. A rule surfacing in prose must already be in
  the bible; a rule in the bible need not ever surface.
- **Costs before powers** for any system (magic, tech, politics): the reader
  trusts a world whose powers cost.
- **As-you-know-Bob** is the tell of failed exposition: characters informing
  each other of what both know. If the reader needs it, find a person who
  genuinely doesn't know it.
- **Time skips**: ground the gap in one concrete line — what changed, what it
  cost — then return to scene.

## Openings and closings (scene and chapter)

Open as late as possible: cut the arrival, the waking, the weather — start on
the first beat that matters. Close on the turn or just after the turn's
landing blow, with the POV character's response begun — not resolved. A chapter
break placed on an unanswered want is the cheapest page-turn device that isn't
a trick.
