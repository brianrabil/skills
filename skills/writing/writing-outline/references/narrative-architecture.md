# Narrative architecture

The structural patterns for [`writing-outline`](../SKILL.md), with what each buys
and what each costs. Choose one deliberately; mix only with a reason you can
state. Subplot nesting and POV rotation come last, since they depend on the
chosen shape.

## The patterns

### Three-act / beat sheet

The default engine of commercial fiction and narrative nonfiction. Act 1 ends at
~25% (commitment to the pursuit), midpoint at ~50% (information shifts the
pursuit's meaning or stakes), act 2 low point at ~75%, climax, new-equilibrium
close. Beat-sheet beat names (Opening Image, Catalyst, Fun and Games, All Is
Lost…) are one useful vocabulary, but the load-bearing joints are the four turns:
what makes the protagonist commit, what makes them pay, what makes them rise.
**Cost:** in unpracticed hands, a template — beats filled by obligation rather
than consequence. If a beat exists only because the sheet lists it, cut it.

### Kishōtenketsu

Four movements: introduction → development → **twist** → reconciliation. No
antagonist and no argument required; the third movement recontextualizes the
first two, and the ending lands because the pieces snap together. Best for
quiet literary fiction, slice of life, associative essays, and any piece whose
material refuses to fight. **Cost:** without a turn that genuinely surprises,
it collapses into a pleasant nothing; test the turn like you would test a
climax — could the reader have seen the ending from the start? Then it is not
kishōtenketsu, it is a description.

### MICE quotient (threads, not acts)

Every story thread is one of: **Milieu** (enter a place → leave it), **Inquiry**
(ask a question → answer it), **Character** (dissatisfaction → change), **Event**
(order broken → restored). Threads **nest**: each opens and must close in
narratively satisfying order. This is the tool for diagnosing "why does the
ending feel off" — usually the wrong thread closed last. Use it to audit any
outline, including nonfiction (a feature is often an Inquiry thread nested in a
Character thread).

### Story spine

`Once upon a time… Every day… Until one day… Because of that… Because of that…
Until finally… And ever since then…` A compression test, not a structure: if you
cannot fill it from your material, the story engine has a hole (usually stakes
or causality). Fastest way to expose an outline full of _and then_.

### Character arcs

- **Positive**: lie → pressure → truth at cost. The want drives scenes; the need
  pays off.
- **Flat**: the character holds the truth and the world resists it. Drama comes
  from testing, not change (most detectives, mentors).
- **Negative / fall**: the lie wins or the truth arrives too late.
  Pick per protagonist up front; a story that starts positive and drifts flat has
  no pressure design.

## Subplots and braiding

Each subplot needs its own thread (want → complications → resolution) and a job
the main plot cannot do: mirror the theme, cost the protagonist something, or
carry a second character's arc. Braid by alternating turns, and let subplot
turns coincide with main-plot turns at the same joints — two changes landing on
one page is free momentum. A subplot that could be deleted without the main plot
missing it is decoration; cut it or give it the job.

## POV threads

One POV per scene, one per chapter for early drafts. Multiple POVs is a promise:
each POV must carry scenes the others cannot, and the rotation should be
rhythmic enough that the reader trusts it. If two POVs see the same events with
no difference in knowledge, one of them is redundant.

## Choosing

- External want vs external antagonist → three-act.
- Quiet, associative, no villain → kishōtenketsu or braided essay.
- Multiple threads and nested questions → MICE to keep nesting sane.
- Unclear which fits → build the story engine first; the engine usually selects
  the shape, and the beat sheet inherits it.
